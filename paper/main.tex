\begin{abstract}
Salient object detection degrades under adverse weather. We ask whether spatial token-level mixture-of-experts (MoE) routing, used without weather labels, helps. On WXSOD the model reaches MAE $0.0192$ on 1{,}500 synthetic and $0.0168$ on 554 real test images, competitive with weather-supervised methods. A full ablation shows the mixture contributes nothing measurable: the no-MoE, one-scale and no-load-balance variants each sit within the seed-noise band of the reference and pass a TOST equivalence test at a margin of $0.001$ MAE, while the dense-expert variant does not. The arms differ from the reference in expert count as well as the named field and share its training seed, so this is not an independent-seed comparison. Entropy sits at $99.8$--$99.97\%$ of its ceiling for the reported model, and at or above $99.2\%$ in every functional arm except the single-expert arm A3 ($97.7\%$ at $1/8$), yet the hard assignment is strongly skewed --- a near-uniform gate coexisting with a consistent ranking. The numbers are not attributable to the mixture; the backbone and decoder were not ablated, so we do not attribute them there either. Section~\ref{sec:why} asks why, and presents ranked hypotheses: the leading one is that the experts are near-identical at initialisation, leaving the gate nothing to separate --- a static selector. The decisive measurements (initial-versus-trained top-2 assignment agreement, and the input-dependence of the routing) have not been made. We state the boundary of what this supports: one backbone, one dataset and one configuration. The equivalence is established; the mechanism is presented as hypothesis rather than fact.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

SOD identifies visually prominent regions as pixel-wise masks, but performance degrades under adverse weather.
Two strategies address this: (1)~weather-label-conditioned models---NIFM~\cite{nifm2025}---require metadata at inference; (2)~MoE for weather restoration---WM-MoE~\cite{wmmoe2023}---targets pixel regression, not segmentation.
Existing MoE-SOD methods primarily explore modality-, scale-, or task-level routing for
multi-modal inputs rather than per-token routing for single-RGB SOD.
We build such a combination --- spatial token-level routing, independent routers at several
pyramid scales, and routing-informed decoding --- for single-RGB SOD without weather
supervision, and the question this paper answers is whether it helps. Sparse MoE has been
studied in dense prediction -- MoE-SPNet routes at the scene level~\cite{moespnet}, token-level
routing has been applied to medical segmentation (SegMoTE~\cite{segmote}), and Pavlitska et
al.\ examine the behaviour of sparse MoE layers in CNN segmentation~\cite{sparsemoecnn} -- and
spatial MoE (SMoE) was introduced for dense regression on spatially-structured scientific data,
such as weather and climate fields~\cite{smoe}. None of these addresses
adverse-weather SOD with a single RGB input.

This paper's central finding is a null result. We designed and trained a spatial MoE that
routes individual tokens to top-$K$ experts at each of three pyramid scales, with per-token
routing entropy fed to the decoder, and it does not measurably affect predictions. The
architecture is competitive --- it reaches MAE $0.0168$ on the real test split without ever
seeing a weather label --- but eleven arms of one ablation family, plus the reported model
and three earlier-recipe arms, and three inference-time interventions show
that the mixture-of-experts, its routing and its entropy channel can each be removed,
randomised or short-circuited with no effect larger than the seed-variance floor
(Sections~\ref{sec:ablations} and~\ref{sec:proxy}). We report the architecture and its numbers
together with the evidence that they are not attributable to routing.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep,label=\arabic*)]
  \item A token-level spatial router that assigns each token to top-$K{=}2$ experts from 8 per scale, using depthwise-convolutional context, noisy top-$K$ gating, and true sparse dispatch.
  \item Three independent routers at scales $1/4$, $1/8$, $1/16$, each with a separate expert pool.
  \item Routing entropy over the full expert distribution, normalized by $\log E$ for $E{=}8$, is injected as an explicit decoder feature via learnable additive fusion.
  \item An ablation and inference-time intervention study (11 trained arms, 3 interventions)
        showing that this routing mechanism is inert.
  \item A candidate explanation of \emph{why} it is inert (Section~\ref{sec:why}), presented as
        ranked hypotheses --- noise-dominated selection at initialisation; auxiliary and Z-loss
        terms that do not reward decisiveness; and experts that are near-identical at
        initialisation, giving the gate nothing to separate and leaving a static selector. Each
        is stated with the evidence bearing on it and the measurement that would discriminate
        it. We keep the equivalence result separate from the mechanism and do not claim the
        mechanism is settled.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Adverse-Weather Salient Object Detection}
Salient object detection under adverse weather is a comparatively recent benchmark setting.
WXSOD~\cite{wxsod2025} supplies the benchmark used here and its own weather-aware baseline,
WFANet, which trains a weather classifier over nine categories and conditions feature fusion
on its output. NIFM~\cite{nifm2025} fuses noise indicators derived from explicit weather-type
one-hot vectors. Both strategies therefore require weather supervision at training time, and
the class-conditioned variant requires it at inference as well. Our model uses no weather
labels at any point; this is the property that makes the comparison in
Section~\ref{sec:compare} meaningful.

\subsection{Mixture-of-Experts for Adverse-Weather Restoration}
Mixture-of-experts has instead been applied to adverse-weather restoration, notably
WM-MoE~\cite{wmmoe2023}, which routes with weather-aware gating and targets pixel regression
rather than segmentation. The distinction matters: restoration is a per-pixel reconstruction
task in which a small residual error is the objective, whereas SOD is a classification-like
segmentation task in which the decision is largely made by the backbone's features. Our
results are consistent with routing capacity not being the binding constraint here, though
that is an observation from one backbone and one dataset rather than a general claim about
segmentation.

\subsection{Mixture-of-Experts in Dense Prediction}
Within dense prediction, MoE-SPNet routes at the scene level~\cite{moespnet};
SegMoTE applies token-level routing to medical image segmentation~\cite{segmote}; and
Pavlitska et al.\ study the behaviour of sparse MoE layers in CNN semantic
segmentation~\cite{sparsemoecnn}. Within SOD specifically, existing MoE methods explore
modality-, scale- or task-level routing for multi-modal inputs rather than per-token routing
for single-RGB imagery.

\subsection{Token-Level Routing and Routing Entropy}
Token-level top-$K$ routing was established by V-MoE~\cite{v-moe2021} for classification,
where routing entropy has also served for uncertainty estimation; we instead inject it as a
decoder feature for dense prediction. Spatial MoE (SMoE) was introduced for dense regression
on spatially-structured scientific data~\cite{smoe}. We apply this combination to single-RGB
adverse-weather SOD, and the
question of whether it helps is one this paper answers in the negative.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} illustrates the full architecture.
A shared PVTv2-B4 backbone extracts multi-scale features; three independent SpatialMoE layers process tokens at each scale; a cross-attention decoder fuses the results and produces dense predictions.
We detail each component below.

%  FIGURE 1 -- ARCHITECTURE OVERVIEW  (left-to-right pipeline)
% ============================================================
\begin{figure}[htbp]

\caption{\textbf{SpatialMoE-SOD.}
A PVTv2-B4 backbone produces features at three resolutions.
Each scale has its \emph{own} Spatial MoE layer: a depthwise-convolutional router turns
every token into eight logits, noisy top-$K{=}2$ sends the token to two of eight experts,
and the experts' outputs are combined with their gate weights.
Per-token routing entropy $\Hs_s$, taken over the full eight-way distribution and
normalised by $\log E$, is projected and added to the routed features.
The cross-attention decoder merges the three scales coarse-to-fine, and two
$1{\times}1$ convolutions produce the saliency and boundary maps.
Eight loss terms are active, including deep supervision over three auxiliary
saliency heads at the $1/16$, $1/8$ and $1/4$ scales.}
\label{fig:arch}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Multi-Scale Features}
A PVTv2-B4 backbone~\cite{pvtv2} pretrained on ImageNet extracts features at three scales via $\texttt{out\_indices}=(0,1,2)$.
Each scale is projected to $C{=}256$ via $1{\times}1$ convolution:
\begin{equation}
  \bX_s = \text{Proj}_s(\text{Backbone}(\bX)_s) \in \reals^{C \times H_s \times W_s},
\end{equation}
where $s \in \{4, 8, 16\}$ with $(H_s,W_s) \in \{(96,96),(48,48),(24,24)\}$.
Each feature map is reshaped into $N_s = H_s \times W_s$ tokens $\bx_i \in \reals^C$.

\subsection{Router}
For each scale, a depthwise $3{\times}3$ convolution captures local spatial context from
the feature map; the full routing mechanism is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:router}.
The router input concatenates each token with its local context:
\begin{equation}
  \br_i = [\bx_i \;|\; \text{DWConv}_{3\times3}(\bX_s)_i] \in \reals^{2C},
\end{equation}
where $|$ denotes channel-wise concatenation, yielding a $2C$-dimensional vector that encodes both the token's content and its spatial neighborhood.
A two-layer MLP produces routing logits:
\begin{equation}
  \bh_i = W_2 \cdot \text{GELU}(W_1 \br_i + b_1) + b_2 \in \reals^{E},
\end{equation}
with $W_1 \in \reals^{128 \times 2C}$, $W_2 \in \reals^{E \times 128}$, $E{=}8$ experts.
The hidden dimension 128 provides a bottleneck between the $2C{=}512$ input and $E{=}8$ output.

%  FIGURE 2 -- ROUTING MECHANISM
% ============================================================
\begin{figure}[htbp]

\caption{\textbf{Routing for one token at one pyramid scale.}
A depthwise $3{\times}3$ convolution supplies local context, which is concatenated with

Input-dependent Gaussian noise is added during training; top-$K{=}2$ then selects two

Each selected expert is a residual MLP with a $4{\times}$ channel expansion.

\end{figure}


\subsection{Noisy Top-$K$ Routing}
During training, Gaussian noise is added with input-dependent scale to encourage exploration:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{h}_{i,j} = h_{i,j} + \epsilon_{i,j}, \quad \epsilon_{i,j} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_j^2(\bx_i)),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_j(\bx_i) = \text{softplus}(w_j^\top \bx_i + b_j)$ is a learned per-expert, per-token noise scale.
At inference, noise is disabled ($\epsilon{=}0$).
Top-$K{=}2$ experts are selected per token; gate weights are softmax over selected logits only:
\begin{equation}
  g_{i,k} = \frac{\exp(\tilde{h}_{i,k})}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{T}_i} \exp(\tilde{h}_{i,j})}, \quad k \in \mathcal{T}_i = \topk_j(\tilde{h}_{i,j}, K{=}2).
\end{equation}
This produces a sparse assignment: each token attends to exactly 2 of 8 experts, and each expert processes only the tokens that selected it.

\subsection{Sparse Expert Aggregation}
Each expert $\E_k$ executes only on tokens that selected it.
For expert $k$, let $\mathcal{A}_k = \{i : k \in \mathcal{T}_i\}$ denote its assigned tokens:
\begin{equation}
  \by_i = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{T}_i} g_{i,k} \cdot \E_k(\bx_i),
\end{equation}
implemented via a loop over experts with mask-based selection (true sparsity, not dense with masking).
Each expert is a token-wise residual MLP:
\begin{equation}
  \E_k(\bx) = \bx + W_k^{(2)} \cdot \text{GELU}(W_k^{(1)} \cdot \text{LN}(\bx)),
\end{equation}
with $W_k^{(1)} \in \reals^{4C \times C}$, $W_k^{(2)} \in \reals^{C \times 4C}$ ($4{\times}$ expansion from $C{=}256$ to $4C{=}1024$).
Each scale has 8 independent experts with no weight sharing (24 total).
Reshaping per-token outputs yields $\bY_s \in \reals^{C \times H_s \times W_s}$.

\subsection{Routing Entropy}
\label{sec:entropy}
Per-token routing entropy measures assignment uncertainty over the full expert distribution:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{H}_i = -\sum_{j=1}^{E} p_{i,j} \log p_{i,j}, \quad \Hs_i = \mathcal{H}_i / \log E \in [0, 1],
\end{equation}
where $p_{i,j}$ is the router's softmax probability for expert $j$ --- computed over all $E$ experts, not only the selected ones.
The normalizing constant is hard-coded to $\log E$, matching the eight-expert model, so $\Hs_i \in [0,1]$: 0 indicates a single expert dominates and 1 a uniform assignment over all eight experts.
The normalized entropy map $\Hs_s \in \reals^{1 \times H_s \times W_s}$ is fused with MoE output via a learned additive block:
\begin{equation}
  \bF_s = \text{Proj}_Y(\bY_s) + \lambda_s \cdot \text{Proj}_\mathcal{H}(\Hs_s),
\end{equation}
where $\text{Proj}_\mathcal{H}$ is a $1{\times}1$ convolution from 1 to $C$ channels and $\lambda_s$ is a learnable scalar initialized to 0.1.
$\Hs_s$ is computed from the router logits and is \emph{not} detached before fusion, so the decoder is a second, direct, differentiable task-gradient path to the router, independent of the expert-difference term in Eq.~(6). It is weak when the gate is near-uniform, because $\partial\Hs/\partial h \to 0$ there, but it is not zero --- a point that matters for the $k{=}1$ arm in Section~\ref{sec:k}.

\subsection{Decoder}
The decoder merges coarse-to-fine features via top-down cross-attention.
At the coarsest scale, $\bF_{16}$ is upsampled and used as queries with $\bF_8$ as keys and values (8-head global attention):
\begin{equation}
  \bF_8' = \text{GlobalCrossAttn}(Q = \text{Up}(\bF_{16}),\; KV = \bF_8).
\end{equation}
Global context is then injected into the fine scale: $\bF_4^{\text{ctx}} = \bF_4 + \text{Proj}_g(\text{AvgPool}(\bF_8'))$.
$\bF_8'$ is upsampled and used as queries with $\bF_4^{\text{ctx}}$ as keys and values using $7{\times}7$ windowed attention for efficiency:
\begin{equation}
  \bF_4' = \text{WinCrossAttn}(Q = \text{Up}(\bF_8'),\; KV = \bF_4^{\text{ctx}}).
\end{equation}
Three refinement blocks (each: Conv $3{\times}3$ $\to$ GroupNorm $\to$ ReLU $\to$ Conv $3{\times}3$ $\to$ GroupNorm $\to$ ReLU $\to$ residual addition) progressively upsample from $1/4$ to full resolution.
Two $1{\times}1$ convolution heads produce saliency logits $\hat{\bM}_{\text{sal}}$ and boundary logits $\hat{\bM}_{\text{bnd}}$ at $384{\times}384$. The decoder additionally exposes three auxiliary saliency heads at the $1/16$, $1/8$ and $1/4$ scales ($\texttt{aux\_logits\_16}$, $\texttt{aux\_logits\_8}$, $\texttt{aux\_logits\_4}$), which the reported configuration enables through deep supervision.

\subsection{Training Objective}
The objective is a weighted sum of the eight terms that are active in the reported configuration
(the Z-loss and the router-confidence term are disabled):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathcal{L} =\;& \lambda_{\text{BCE}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}
  + \lambda_{\text{IoU}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{IoU}}
  + \lambda_{\text{SSIM}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{SSIM}}
  + \lambda_{\text{BND}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BND}}\\
  &+ \lambda_{\text{LB}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{LB}}
  + \lambda_{\text{IMP}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{IMP}}
  + \lambda_{\text{auxBND}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{auxBND}}
  + \lambda_{\text{DS}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DS}},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\text{BCE}}{=}\lambda_{\text{IoU}}{=}\lambda_{\text{SSIM}}{=}\lambda_{\text{BND}}{=}1.0$,
$\lambda_{\text{LB}}{=}\lambda_{\text{IMP}}{=}0.01$, $\lambda_{\text{auxBND}}{=}0.5$ and $\lambda_{\text{DS}}{=}0.4$ ($\lambda_{\text{LB}}$ is applied at each of the three scales).
These weights describe the \emph{reported} model. The ablation-family arms of
Section~\ref{sec:ablations} share the architecture but not the router-loss configuration: they
were re-run under the current code with per-scale load-balance weights $0.08/0.15/0.10$,
$\lambda_{\text{IMP}}{=}0.05$ and $\lambda_{\text{Z}}{=}0.001$. So this Method section does not
describe the ablation model exactly, and the difference matters for the router: the Z-loss
penalises $\log\sum_j e^{h_j}$ and therefore pushes router logits towards zero, which drives
the gate softmax towards uniform. That makes it a direct candidate cause of the flat routing
entropy examined in Section~\ref{sec:why}.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}$ is binary cross-entropy with logits between predicted saliency logits and ground-truth mask.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{IoU}}$ is the soft IoU loss:
$1 - \text{mean}_b\!\left[\frac{\sum_{hw}\sigma(\hat{M}_b) \cdot M_b + \epsilon}{\sum_{hw}\sigma(\hat{M}_b) + \sum_{hw} M_b - \sum_{hw}\sigma(\hat{M}_b) \cdot M_b + \epsilon}\right]$.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{SSIM}}$ is $1$ minus the structural-similarity index between the sigmoid prediction and the mask.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BND}}$ is a smooth-$L_1$ term between the magnitude of the predicted spatial gradient and the ground-truth boundary map.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{LB}}$ is the load-balancing loss:
$\sum_{s} E \sum_j f_j^{(s)} P_j^{(s)}$, summed over the three scales, where $f_j$ is the fraction of all routing slots assigned to expert $j$, computed from hard assignments and detached, and $P_j$ is the differentiable mean gate mass.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{IMP}}$ is the importance loss:
$\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{s} (\text{std}(\bP^{(s)}) / \text{mean}(\bP^{(s)}))^2$, where $\bP^{(s)} \in \reals^E$ denotes the per-expert mean gate-mass vector at scale $s$.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{auxBND}}$ is binary cross-entropy between the boundary head's logits and the boundary map.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DS}}$ is the mean binary cross-entropy of the three per-scale auxiliary saliency heads. Those three $1{\times}1$ convolutions are the only parameters deep supervision adds, accounting for the difference between the 69{,}212{,}349-parameter model that omits them and the reported 69{,}213{,}120-parameter model.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

\subsection{Setup}
We evaluate on WXSOD~\cite{wxsod2025}, which provides 12,891 training images (scene-aware 80/20 split), 1,500 synthetic test images across 9 weather categories, and 554 real-world test images across 5 categories (fog, rain, snow, dark, light).
Images are aspect-preserving resized to $384{\times}384$; augmentations include horizontal flip ($p{=}0.5$), color jitter ($p{=}0.5$), and ImageNet normalization.
Training uses DDP on 2 GPUs, AMP (FP16), AdamW optimizer (lr $10^{-4}$ for both newly introduced modules and the backbone, weight decay $10^{-4}$), a WarmupCosine schedule, gradient clipping at 1.0 and effective batch size 32; the backbone is frozen during the first epoch and unfrozen from epoch 1 onward.
Two schedules were used, and this matters for reading every comparison below. The reported model \textbf{R} was configured for a 50-epoch cosine with $1\%$ warmup and early-stopped at 14 epochs, so its learning rate had \emph{not} annealed when its numbers were taken. The ablation-family arms were configured for 8 epochs (14 for A1) with a $4\%$ warmup, so they did anneal within their own, shorter schedules. Neither is a converged 50-epoch run, and \textbf{R}'s headline numbers are not reproduced by any ablation-family arm.
Model selection for \textbf{R} is by validation MAE on the scene-disjoint validation split, so no test split enters it. \textbf{R} is evaluated single-pass, whereas the ablation-family arms use flip-average TTA; a flip-TTA evaluation of \textbf{R} also exists (MAE $0.0167$) but is not the reported row, so the \textbf{R}-versus-\textbf{Ref} rows mix TTA settings.
All evaluation numbers are scored in original image coordinates: predictions are reverse-geometried out of the $384{\times}384$ padded model frame before any metric is computed.

\noindent\textbf{Metrics.} We report MAE and the structure measure $S_\phi$~\cite{struct-measure}, the enhanced-alignment measure $E_\phi^{\text{adp}}$~\cite{enhanced-measure} and the maximum F-measure $F_\beta^{\max}$~\cite{f-measure}, together with boundary MAE and boundary F1. MAE is the mean absolute difference between the continuous prediction and the binary ground truth. For the threshold sweeps of Fig.~\ref{fig:pr}, given a threshold $t$ and the corresponding binary mask, we report precision $P(t)$, recall $R(t)$ and $F_\beta(t) = (1+\beta^2)PR/(\beta^2 P + R)$ with $\beta^2{=}0.3$, computed per image and averaged over the split.

The model has 69.21M parameters and 277.9G MACs per $384{\times}384$ input image; the split by component is given in Table~\ref{tab:cost}.

\noindent\textbf{What we find.} Four results, each backed by a table below.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=2pt,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{The mixture-of-experts does not change the prediction.} Removing it entirely
        leaves real-test MAE at $0.0195$, identical to the full model, and routing at one
        pyramid scale instead of three gives the same $0.0195$ (Table~\ref{tab:ablate}).
  \item \textbf{Competitive without weather labels.} MAE $0.0192$ on the 1{,}500 synthesized
        test images leads every method in the published benchmark, including the
        weather-supervised WFANet ($0.0229$); $0.0168$ on the 554 real images is second of
        nineteen (Table~\ref{tab:compare}, Fig.~\ref{fig:ranking}).
  \item \textbf{The router's gate distribution is nearly uniform.} Routing entropy sits at
        $99.2\%$ or more of its $\log E$ ceiling in every functional arm except the
        single-expert arm A3 ($97.7\%$ at $1/8$), so the decoder's entropy channel is nearly
        constant (Tables~\ref{tab:entropy} and~\ref{tab:diag}).
  \item \textbf{Training budget matters more than any single design choice.} The same recipe at 14
        epochs instead of 8 improves real-test MAE from $0.0195$ to $0.0174$, against a seed
        spread of $\pm0.0004$ (Tables~\ref{tab:ablate} and~\ref{tab:seeds}).
\end{itemize}

The model achieves MAE of $0.0192$ on synthetic data and $0.0168$ on real-world data.

\subsection{Ablations}
\label{sec:ablations}
Table~\ref{tab:ablate} reports every arm we trained; arm codes are defined in the caption.
The middle block shares one training recipe --- router noise enabled, effective batch 32,
8 epochs --- and each row changes one field relative to the reference \textbf{Ref}, with two
exceptions we state plainly: A3 changes $E$ and $k$ together, and the A2--A6 arms were run at
$E{=}4$ or $E{=}2$ while \textbf{Ref} uses $E{=}8$, so expert count varies within the block and
the $E{=}4$, $k{=}2$ reference arm was never run. Rows within the block are comparable only
with those differences in mind.

Three findings stand out.
First, \textbf{removing the mixture-of-experts entirely leaves performance unchanged}: the
no-MoE arm scores 0.0195 on real test against 0.0195 for the full model, with $S_\phi$
agreeing to 0.0010.
Second, \textbf{reducing routing from three pyramid scales to one changes nothing} (0.0195),
and replacing the routed experts with a single dense expert per scale leaves the point estimate
unchanged (0.0191) but is the one architectural arm the paired test does not certify as
equivalent (Table~\ref{tab:equiv}).
Third, the one arm that clearly separates is A3, the single-expert arm: it costs $0.0017$ MAE
($0.0212$ against $0.0195$), roughly four times the seed spread of $\pm0.0004$ measured across
seeds 42--44. A3 changes both $E$ and $k$ (Section~\ref{sec:k}), so it does not isolate top-$k$.

(The A2, A4 and A5 point estimates of $0.0002$, $0.0001$ and $0.0004$ sit inside that band; the
paired equivalence test of Table~\ref{tab:equiv} certifies A2, A4 and A6 as equivalent but not
A5, whose $0.0005$ difference is not resolvable either way at this sample size.)

That floor is measured on the reference configuration, and the remaining arms are single-seed,
so their deltas are compared against a floor estimated elsewhere. A paired bootstrap over
per-image errors, with a TOST equivalence test, is reported in Table~\ref{tab:equiv}; it tests
against a margin of $\pm0.001$ rather than against that seed floor, and the arms share the
reference's seed, so it is not an independent comparison.

\textbf{Training budget is the largest single effect we measured}: the
same recipe and seed trained for 14 epochs instead of 8 improves real-test MAE from 0.0195
to 0.0174, larger than any individual architectural difference (the largest, A3, is $0.0017$)
though not larger than their sum, and confounded with the number of optimizer steps.
We therefore report the architecture as characterisation, not as a demonstrated improvement.

Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative} renders the no-mixture comparison as an image: the full model and
the arm with the mixture removed produce visually identical masks on the same scenes.

\subsection{Discussion}
The model demonstrates that spatial token-level routing is feasible for single-RGB SOD without weather-specific supervision.
However, several important limitations must be noted.
First and most centrally, the spatial MoE is shown across the eleven arms of the ablation family and three
inference-time interventions to have no measurable effect on output quality; we treat that as
the paper's primary contribution rather than a caveat. Routing entropy sits at or above
$99.2\%$ of its ceiling everywhere except the single-expert arm, so the decoder's entropy
channel is nearly constant.
Second, the comparison against prior methods uses the benchmark's published numbers rather than our own re-implementations, so it is a comparison as reported; WFANet still attains the best real-split MAE, so we do not claim state-of-the-art performance.
Third, the reported boundary metrics are computed from the binarised saliency mask's edge
against a thin ground-truth band at zero tolerance, not from the boundary head; F1 is $0.3601$
on real test under that convention, and the boundary head's own accuracy is not measured here.
Fourth, the model was trained once without replicate runs, so variance estimates for the
reported configuration are unavailable.
Fifth, the forced-expert analysis suggests that expert specialization may be limited, and the benefit of multi-expert routing over a single larger expert remains unclear.

\section{Why the Router May Be Inert: Candidate Mechanisms}
\label{sec:why}
% ============================================================

The ablation of Section~\ref{sec:ablations} and the interventions of Section~\ref{sec:proxy}
establish \emph{that} the router does not matter measurably, at this recipe. This section asks
\emph{why}. We keep the
result and the explanation separate: the equivalence of the ablations is a finding, while the
mechanism below is a ranked set of hypotheses, none of them established by a direct measurement
of the router.

\subsection{Routing as a Sparse Selection Operator}
Let $\bX \in \reals^{N \times C}$ be the token matrix at one scale and
$\E_j \,:\, \reals^{C} \to \reals^{C}$ the $j$-th expert. Write
$\bPi \in \{0,1\}^{N \times E}$ for the selection matrix induced by top-$K$,
$\Pi_{ij} = \mathbb{1}[\,j \in \mathcal{T}_i\,]$,
and let $\bg$ be the $N \times E$ matrix of gate weights, supported on the selected experts.
The layer is then exactly
\begin{equation}
  \by_i \;=\; \sum_{j=1}^{E} \bigl(\Pi_{ij}\, g_{ij}\bigr)\, \E_j(\bx_i)
  \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
  \bY = \bigl(\bPi \odot \bg\bigr) \cdot \E(\bX),
\end{equation}
where $\odot$ is the Hadamard product and $\E(\bX) \in \reals^{N \times E \times C}$ stacks
the expert outputs. The routing enters the output only through the weighted selection
$\bPi \odot \bg$; what that weighting acts on is the expert \emph{difference}, which is where
Section~\ref{sec:hyp} begins.

\subsection{The Router Is at Its Entropy Ceiling From Initialisation}
\label{sec:info}
The entropy of the gate distribution is the natural first quantity to inspect, but it cannot
carry the argument on its own. Table~\ref{tab:entropy} measures $\Hs \in [0.9981, 0.9997]$ for
the reported model, so the gate distribution is within a few millibits of uniform \emph{in
magnitude}. Two caveats apply before any conclusion is drawn. First, magnitude is not ranking:
top-$K$ selection depends only on the \emph{ranking} of the logits, and Table~\ref{tab:sim}
shows that ranking is structured --- at $1/8$ three experts take about $84\%$ of the hard
assignments and two are dead. Second, any entropy functional has \emph{zero} gradient at the
uniform fixed point, so its inability to move the distribution is expected rather than
informative. Entropy alone therefore establishes neither indifference nor failure to train.

\paragraph{Initialisation.}
Using the checkpoint's own configuration we build fresh models under five init seeds and read
the normalised routing entropy at each scale from \texttt{MoEOutput.entropy}. At initialisation
the mean over seeds is $0.9991$, $0.9994$ and $0.9993$ of the $\log E$ ceiling at $1/16$, $1/8$
and $1/4$. The router \emph{begins} at its ceiling.

\paragraph{Training.}
The reported checkpoint's own diagnostic pass gives normalised entropies of $0.9997$, $0.9997$
and $0.9982$ at $1/4$, $1/8$ and $1/16$ (Table~\ref{tab:diag}); a separate forensic pass over
the same recipe on real-test tokens gives $0.9998$, $0.9999$ and $0.9968$. Relative to
initialisation the entropy rises slightly at $1/4$ and $1/8$ and falls at $1/16$; the one scale
that moved is the one that sharpened. What this shows is that the \emph{magnitude} of the gate
distribution barely changes. It does not show that the \emph{top-2 assignment} is unchanged,
because a router can reshuffle which experts it selects with almost no change in entropy; the
measurement that would settle it --- agreement between the initial and trained top-2 assignments
on identical tokens --- has not been made.

\paragraph{Consequence.}
If the router contributes approximately an unweighted mean of whichever experts it selects,
deleting the mixture should cost nothing, and it does not: arm A6, in which the MoE is disabled
outright, is statistically equivalent to the full model within $\pm 0.001$ MAE
(Table~\ref{tab:equiv}; TOST $p = 0.0001$, paired bootstrap over the 554 test images), as are
A2, A4 and the routing-scope variant, subject to the expert-count difference noted in
Section~\ref{sec:ablations}. That equivalence is the paper's firm result. The explanation is
not, and Section~\ref{sec:hyp} ranks the candidates.

\subsection{Why the Single-Expert Arm Is the One That Separates}
\label{sec:k}
Most architectural variants in Table~\ref{tab:equiv} are equivalent to the reference; the one
that separates in the opposite direction is A3, which selects a single expert and moves MAE to
$0.0212$ against $0.0195$. Two properties of that arm are structural rather than incidental.

First, with one selected expert the gate weight is not a softmax over a choice but the constant
$1$: renormalising over a single logit leaves $1$ regardless of that logit's value. The layer
therefore computes one expert's output rather than a mean of two, and the averaging that would
suppress per-expert noise is absent.

Second --- and this is why $k{=}1$ differs from every other knob --- the gate receives no
gradient through the \emph{mixture}: with one selected expert the gate is the constant $1$, so
the sum over selected experts has a single term and no difference $\E_a - \E_b$ survives to
carry a signal. That is not the same as receiving no task gradient at all. The router logits are
still differentiable inputs to the decoder through the entropy channel
(Section~\ref{sec:entropy}), which is not detached, and to the load-balance, importance and Z
terms; the entropy path is weak for a near-uniform router because
$\partial\Hs/\partial h \to 0$ at uniformity, but it is not zero. The correct statement is that
$k{=}1$ removes the mixture's own path to the router, not that the router is frozen.

We state the confound plainly: A3 was built at $E{=}4$ with $k{=}1$, while the reference and the
other four-expert arms use $k{=}2$, so this arm varies $E$ and $k$ together and does not isolate
$k$. The study also contains an $E{=}2$, $k{=}1$ run, but it diverged ($0.0546$;
Table~\ref{tab:ablate}, B3) and is not evidence about $k$. Neither arm separates $k$ from $E$ or
from a training failure; a matched $E{=}2$, $k{=}2$ versus $E{=}2$, $k{=}1$ pair at identical
settings is what would isolate $k$, and we have not run one.

\subsection{The Auxiliary Routing Losses Do Not Reward Decisiveness}
Two of the auxiliary terms in Eq.~(12) are \emph{minimised by a uniform gate distribution}, and
a third --- active in the ablation recipe but not the reported one --- penalises logit
magnitude and therefore also pushes the gate towards uniform. They are a candidate explanation
for the flat gate, though the ablation evidence below cuts against their being the main cause.

For the Shazeer load-balancing term, let $f_j$ be the (detached) hard assignment fraction and
$P_j$ the differentiable mean gate mass, with $\sum_j f_j = \sum_j P_j = 1$. Because $f$ is
computed from the very assignments that produce $P$, the two are positively coupled, and under
that coupling $\sum_j f_j P_j$ is minimised when both are uniform:
\begin{equation}
  \sum_{j=1}^{E} f_j P_j \;\ge\; E \cdot \frac{1}{E}\cdot\frac{1}{E} \;=\; \frac{1}{E},
\end{equation}
with equality iff $f = P = u$. This is Chebyshev's inequality, so it requires $f$ and $P$
to be similarly ordered; they are, since both are computed from the same assignments, but the
bound would fail for anti-correlated $f$ and $P$. A concentrated routing table gives
$\sum_j f_j P_j \to 1$, i.e. $E$ times the minimum. The gradient of $\mathcal{L}_{\text{LB}}$
with respect to the router parameters is $E\sum_j f_j \,\partial_\theta P_j$, which is non-zero
for any non-uniform $P$ and drives the gate mass \emph{toward} uniform.

The importance term,
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{IMP}} = \bigl(\mathrm{std}(\bP)/\mathrm{mean}(\bP)\bigr)^2$, is likewise
non-negative and exactly zero at uniform $\bP$. Neither term rewards \emph{decisiveness}; both
penalise deviation from uniformity.

A third term is stronger still. The ablation-family recipe (Section~\ref{sec:method}) enables a
Z-loss, $\mathcal{L}_{\text{Z}} = \bigl(\log\sum_j e^{h_j}\bigr)^2$ with
$\lambda_{\text{Z}}{=}0.001$, which the reported model disables. It is minimised by small router
logits, and small logits make the softmax flat, so it pulls the gate towards uniform directly
rather than merely failing to reward confidence.

The ablation evidence cuts against these terms being the main cause. Arm A4 removes the
load-balance term (keeping the importance and Z terms at their family weights) and the router is
\emph{still} essentially uniform ($\Hs = 0.9996$, $0.9998$, $0.9969$ at $1/4$, $1/8$, $1/16$),
so removing load balance does not make the gate more decisive; and the reported model R has no
Z-loss and is equally flat. A run with $\lambda_{\text{Z}}{=}0$ at the family recipe is the clean
test of the Z-loss and has not been run.

Two limits apply. The load-balance and importance terms act on the batch-mean gate mass $P_j$,
not on each token's own distribution, so a router could be balanced on average while every token
is decisive, which is how such losses are normally used; per-token evidence would be needed to
tie them to per-token uniformity. And none of these terms is shown here to \emph{cause} the flat
router: taken with the initialisation measurement, the defensible reading is that the objective
contains no reward for decisiveness and at least one term that actively suppresses logit
magnitude.

\subsection{Ranked Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hyp}
Three mechanisms could leave the router near-uniform and unchanging. We state them with the
evidence already in hand --- suggestive, and confounded by recipe differences --- and the
measurement that would discriminate each.

\textbf{(H1) Selection is noise-dominated at initialisation.} Noisy top-$K$ adds Gaussian noise
with a learned scale $\sigma_j(\bx_i) = \mathrm{softplus}(w_j^\top\bx_i + b_j)$, of order $0.7$
at initialisation against logits of order $0.1$; training-time selection would then be close to
random. \emph{Evidence against:} the two noise-off batch-40 arms sit at $1.0000$ at $1/4$ and
$1/8$, flatter than \textbf{Ref} with noise on, so removing the noise does not make the gate
more structured. Confound: those arms also change the batch and gate mode.

\textbf{(H2) The auxiliary and Z-loss terms do not reward decisiveness.} The load-balance and
importance terms are minimised at a uniform gate, and the ablation recipe's Z-loss penalises
logit magnitude. \emph{Evidence against their being the main cause:} arm A4 removes the
load-balance term and the router stays equally flat, so load balance is not what makes it flat;
and the reported model \textbf{R} has no Z-loss yet is equally flat, so the Z-loss is not the
main cause either. They remain at most contributors.

\textbf{(H3) The experts are near-identical at initialisation, leaving the gate nothing to
separate.} The mixture's own signal is $g_{ij}(\E_a - \E_b)$; with residual experts the shared
$\bx$ cancels, so the signal is the difference of two MLP branches, which is $\approx 0$ if the
experts start alike. The gate is then a \emph{static selector}: small logits with a consistent,
possibly token-independent ranking. Two observations support this and are not explained by
gradient starvation. First, the hard usage is strongly skewed with dead experts in nearly every
arm, which a flat full-softmax entropy does not predict. Second, and opposite to a starvation
story, arm A3 --- the arm with the least gradient through the mixture --- is the \emph{least}
flat ($0.9766$ at $1/8$, with dead experts); this is one arm, confounded with expert count, so
more starvation need not mean more flatness.
Note that the dense-gate arm A7 being flat (Table~\ref{tab:diag}) is \emph{consistent} with H3,
not evidence against it: a dense gate still multiplies the same expert difference. The discriminating measurements
are the input-dependence of the assignment (decomposing the logit variance into
token-independent and token-dependent parts, and the mutual information of the assignment with
weather class and with spatial position), the initial-versus-trained top-2 agreement on
identical tokens, and oracle routing by weather class as a positive control. None has been run.

None of the three is established; all three predict a near-uniform gate, and each is confounded.
We report them as ranked hypotheses rather than a settled mechanism.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}
% ============================================================

The results in this paper are one training run per configuration, and the evidence for the
null result rests on deltas that are small in absolute terms. We state the consequences.

\emph{The equivalence test is not an architectural comparison.} Table~\ref{tab:equiv} reports a
paired bootstrap and a TOST against a margin of $\pm0.001$ MAE chosen larger than the
$\pm0.0004$ seed floor and fixed after the measurements were in hand. The test fails to certify
the two seed replicates ($p=0.057$, $p=0.051$) while certifying architecturally different arms
that share the reference's seed 42, so it is measuring the shared per-image error structure at
least as much as the architecture. The clean benchmark is a seed-versus-seed comparison; until
each arm is trained under independent seeds, these equivalence claims are provisional.

\emph{The interventions cannot separate ``useless'' from ``untrained''.} The inference-time
perturbations (Table~\ref{tab:proxy}) replace or disable the router in a model trained with noisy
routing; if selection was already noise-dominated during training (H1), then random routing at
inference is in-distribution, and its small effect is expected. The interventions become
informative only once a model has been trained with routing noise off.

\emph{The equivalence comparison does not use the reported recipe.} \textbf{R} is the
earlier-recipe model and was never re-run against a matched no-MoE control; its closest
ablation-family counterpart, A1, differs from it by $0.0010$ synthetic and $0.0006$ real, larger
than anything the paper treats as noise. A three-seed no-MoE versus full-model comparison at
\textbf{R}'s recipe, and at 14 epochs, is what would turn single-run equivalence into a real
comparison, and it has not been run.

\emph{The comparison is as reported.} Every baseline number in Table~\ref{tab:compare} and
Fig.~\ref{fig:ranking} is the published authors' own measurement under their own protocol, not
a re-run in this codebase. The one protocol difference we can state is the input convention
(both score in original image coordinates). WFANet retains the best real-split MAE, so no
state-of-the-art claim is made.

\emph{The decoder and the backbone were not ablated.} All arms share a PVTv2-B4 backbone, a
cross-attention decoder and the eight-term objective, so attributing performance to ``the
backbone'' really attributes it to the backbone, decoder and loss together; nothing here
separates them.

\emph{The $E{=}4$, $k{=}2$ reference arm was never run}, so expert count is bracketed by
$E{=}2$ and $E{=}8$ rather than measured at the midpoint.

\emph{One scale of the routing was probed.} The forced-expert intervention was applied only to
the $1/4$ scale; the effect of forcing the other two scales is unknown.

\emph{The boundary head is not measured.} The reported boundary metrics come from the binarised
saliency mask, not the boundary head ($0.3601$ F1 on real test under a zero-tolerance band
overlap), so we cannot say whether the head is weak or how much it contributes.

\emph{The mechanism is a hypothesis.} The measurements establish equivalence; they do not
establish why. None of the three hypotheses of Section~\ref{sec:hyp} has its discriminating
measurement: neither the input-dependence of the routing nor the initial-versus-trained top-2
agreement has been computed, and none of the noise-off, small-$\sigma$, zero-Z-loss or
oracle-routing runs has been performed.

\emph{Training-budget effects are unstudied.} The 14-epoch versus 8-epoch comparison is a
single pair of runs and confounds epoch count with the number of optimizer steps; we report it
as the largest effect observed, not as an established scaling law.

% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ============================================================

We presented and ablated SpatialMoE-SOD. On WXSOD the model reaches MAE $0.0168$ on the 554
real test images and $0.0192$ on the 1{,}500 synthetic ones. A complete ablation and
inference-time intervention study shows the mixture-of-experts is not responsible for those
numbers: it can be removed, reduced to one pyramid scale, or replaced with a single dense expert,
and the no-MoE, one-scale and no-load-balance variants each sit within the seed-noise band of
the reference and pass a TOST equivalence test against it at a margin of $0.001$ MAE (the
dense-expert variant does not), though the arms differ from the reference in expert count as
well as the named field and share its seed. We do not
attribute the performance to the backbone or the decoder either, because neither was ablated;
the supportable claim is that it is not attributable to the mixture, at this one recipe.
Fourteen epochs instead of 8 exceeds every architectural effect we measured.

The paper is deliberately two-sided. Empirically, it reports weather-label-free numbers
together with the ablation showing that the mixture does not produce them. Analytically, it asks
why the routing is inert and ranks three candidates: selection that is noise-dominated at
initialisation; auxiliary and Z-loss terms that do not reward decisiveness; and experts that are
near-identical at initialisation, leaving the gate nothing to separate and making it a static
selector. We do not present the mechanism as settled:
the measurements that would separate the three --- the input-dependence of the routing, the
initial-versus-trained top-2 agreement, and oracle routing as a positive control --- have not
been made. We claim nothing beyond this configuration.

Removing the mixture, reducing routing to one scale, replacing routed experts with a dense
expert and removing the load-balancing loss all leave
real-test MAE within the seed-noise band of the reference, while routing to a single expert
costs $0.0017$. The mixture is not earning its parameters on this task, and the useful direction
is not more routing capacity but the training budget.

Future work should run the experiments that discriminate among the candidate mechanisms ---
noise off, a small initial $\sigma$, zero Z-loss, a router learning-rate multiplier, and oracle
routing by weather class as a positive control --- add a multi-seed no-MoE versus full-model
comparison at \textbf{R}'s recipe, and re-run the benchmark's baselines under a single unified
protocol.
