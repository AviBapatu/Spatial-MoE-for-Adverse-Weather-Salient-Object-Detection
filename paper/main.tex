\section{Introduction}

SOD identifies visually prominent regions as pixel-wise masks, but performance degrades under adverse weather.
Two strategies address this: (1)~weather-label-conditioned models---NIFM~\cite{nifm2025}---require metadata at inference; (2)~MoE for weather restoration---WM-MoE~\cite{wmmoe2023}---targets pixel regression, not segmentation.
To our knowledge, existing MoE-SOD methods primarily explore modality-, scale-, or task-level routing for multi-modal inputs rather than per-token routing for single-RGB SOD.
To our knowledge no prior work combines spatial token-level routing, independent routers at
several pyramid scales, and routing-informed decoding for single-RGB SOD without weather
supervision. Sparse MoE has been studied in dense prediction -- MoE-SPNet routes at the scene
level~\cite{moespnet}, token-level routing has been applied to medical segmentation
(SegMoTE~\cite{segmote}), and Pavlitska et al.\ examine the behaviour of sparse MoE layers in
CNN segmentation~\cite{sparsemoecnn} -- and spatial MoE has been applied in a classification
setting~\cite{smoe}. None of these addresses adverse-weather SOD with a single RGB input.
This paper's central finding is a null result: the spatial MoE we designed does not measurably
affect predictions (Sections~IV-F, IV-G). We report the architecture and its numbers together
with the evidence that they are not attributable to routing.

Our contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep,label=\arabic*)]
  \item A token-level spatial router that assigns each token to top-$K{=}2$ experts from 8 per scale, using depthwise-convolutional context, noisy top-$K$ gating, and true sparse dispatch.
  \item Three independent routers at scales $1/4$, $1/8$, $1/16$, each with a separate expert pool.
  \item Routing entropy over the full expert distribution, normalized by $\log 8$ for $E{=}8$, is injected as an explicit decoder feature via learnable additive fusion.
  \item An ablation and inference-time intervention study (11 trained arms, 4 perturbations)
        showing that this routing mechanism is inert.
\end{enumerate}

Code, configurations and the per-run evaluation artifacts are available at~\cite{artifact2026}.

The rest of the paper is organised as follows.
Section~II positions the work against adverse-weather SOD and mixture-of-experts methods.
Section~III describes the architecture: the multi-scale backbone, the spatial router, sparse
dispatch, the entropy-fused decoder and the training objective.
Section~IV reports the results --- the headline numbers, a comparison against published
methods, and a controlled ablation suite --- and explains why the ablation behaves as it does.
Section~V states what the evidence supports and what it does not.

\section{Related Work}
Two strategies address SOD under adverse weather. Weather-label-conditioned models such as
NIFM~\cite{nifm2025} fuse noise indicators from explicit weather-type one-hot vectors, and
WXSOD~\cite{wxsod2025} supplies the benchmark and its own weather-aware baseline; both need
weather supervision. Mixture-of-experts has instead been applied to adverse-weather restoration,
notably WM-MoE~\cite{wmmoe2023}, which routes with weather-aware gating and targets pixel
regression rather than segmentation. Within SOD, existing MoE methods explore modality-, scale-
or task-level routing for multi-modal inputs rather than per-token routing for single-RGB
imagery. Token-level top-$K$ routing was established by V-MoE~\cite{v-moe2021} for
classification, where routing entropy has also served for uncertainty estimation; we inject it
as a decoder feature for dense prediction.

\section{Method}

Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} illustrates the full architecture.
A shared PVTv2-B4 backbone extracts multi-scale features; three independent SpatialMoE layers process tokens at each scale; a cross-attention decoder fuses the results and produces dense predictions.
We detail each component below.

\subsection{Multi-Scale Features}
A PVTv2-B4 backbone~\cite{pvtv2} pretrained on ImageNet extracts features at three scales via $\texttt{out\_indices}=(0,1,2)$.
Each scale is projected to $C{=}256$ via $1{\times}1$ convolution:
\begin{equation}
  \bX_s = \text{Proj}_s(\text{Backbone}(\bX)_s) \in \reals^{C \times H_s \times W_s},
\end{equation}
where $s \in \{4, 8, 16\}$ with $(H_s,W_s) \in \{(96,96),(48,48),(24,24)\}$.
Each feature map is reshaped into $N_s = H_s \times W_s$ tokens $\bx_i \in \reals^C$.

\subsection{Router}
For each scale, a depthwise $3{\times}3$ convolution captures local spatial context from
the feature map; the full routing mechanism is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:router}.
The router input concatenates each token with its local context:
\begin{equation}
  \br_i = [\bx_i \;|\; \text{DWConv}_{3\times3}(\bX_s)_i] \in \reals^{2C},
\end{equation}
where $|$ denotes channel-wise concatenation, yielding a $2C$-dimensional vector that encodes both the token's content and its spatial neighborhood.
A two-layer MLP produces routing logits:
\begin{equation}
  \bh_i = W_2 \cdot \text{GELU}(W_1 \br_i + b_1) + b_2 \in \reals^{E},
\end{equation}
with $W_1 \in \reals^{128 \times 2C}$, $W_2 \in \reals^{E \times 128}$, $E{=}8$ experts.
The hidden dimension 128 provides a bottleneck between the $2C{=}512$ input and $E{=}8$ output.

\subsection{Noisy Top-$K$ Routing}
During training, Gaussian noise is added with input-dependent scale to encourage exploration:
\begin{equation}
  \tilde{h}_{i,j} = h_{i,j} + \epsilon_{i,j}, \quad \epsilon_{i,j} \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma_j^2(\bx_i)),
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_j(\bx_i) = \text{softplus}(w_j^\top \bx_i + b_j)$ is a learned per-expert, per-token noise scale.
At inference, noise is disabled ($\epsilon{=}0$).
Top-$K{=}2$ experts are selected per token; gate weights are softmax over selected logits only:
\begin{equation}
  g_{i,k} = \frac{\exp(\tilde{h}_{i,k})}{\sum_{j \in \mathcal{T}_i} \exp(\tilde{h}_{i,j})}, \quad k \in \mathcal{T}_i = \topk_j(\tilde{h}_{i,j}, K{=}2).
\end{equation}
This produces a sparse assignment: each token attends to exactly 2 of 8 experts, and each expert processes only the tokens that selected it.

\subsection{Sparse Expert Aggregation}
Each expert $\E_k$ executes only on tokens that selected it.
For expert $k$, let $\mathcal{A}_k = \{i : k \in \mathcal{T}_i\}$ denote its assigned tokens:
\begin{equation}
  \by_i = \sum_{k \in \mathcal{T}_i} g_{i,k} \cdot \E_k(\bx_i),
\end{equation}
implemented via a loop over experts with mask-based selection (true sparsity, not dense with masking).
Each expert is a token-wise residual MLP:
\begin{equation}
  \E_k(\bx) = \bx + W_k^{(2)} \cdot \text{GELU}(W_k^{(1)} \cdot \text{LN}(\bx)),
\end{equation}
with $W_k^{(1)} \in \reals^{4C \times C}$, $W_k^{(2)} \in \reals^{C \times 4C}$ ($4{\times}$ expansion from $C{=}256$ to $4C{=}1024$).
Each scale has 8 independent experts with no weight sharing (24 total).
Reshaping per-token outputs yields $\bY_s \in \reals^{C \times H_s \times W_s}$.

\subsection{Routing Entropy}
Per-token routing entropy measures assignment uncertainty over the full expert distribution:
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{H}_i = -\sum_{j=1}^{E} p_{i,j} \log p_{i,j}, \quad \Hs_i = \mathcal{H}_i / \log 8 \in [0, 1],
\end{equation}
where $p_{i,j}$ is the router's softmax probability for expert $j$ --- computed over all $E$ experts, not only the selected ones.
The normalizing constant is hard-coded to $\log 8$, matching the eight-expert model, so $\Hs_i \in [0,1]$: 0 indicates a single expert dominates and 1 a uniform assignment over all eight experts.
The normalized entropy map $\Hs_s \in \reals^{1 \times H_s \times W_s}$ is fused with MoE output via a learned additive block:
\begin{equation}
  \bF_s = \text{Proj}_Y(\bY_s) + \lambda_s \cdot \text{Proj}_\mathcal{H}(\Hs_s),
\end{equation}
where $\text{Proj}_\mathcal{H}$ is a $1{\times}1$ convolution from 1 to $C$ channels and $\lambda_s$ is a learnable scalar initialized to 0.1.
This allows the decoder to condition its attention on routing confidence without requiring the entropy to be differentiable through the expert weights.

\subsection{Decoder}
The decoder merges coarse-to-fine features via top-down cross-attention.
At the coarsest scale, $\bF_{16}$ is upsampled and used as queries with $\bF_8$ as keys and values (8-head global attention):
\begin{equation}
  \bF_8' = \text{GlobalCrossAttn}(Q = \text{Up}(\bF_{16}),\; KV = \bF_8).
\end{equation}
Global context is then injected into the fine scale: $\bF_4^{\text{ctx}} = \bF_4 + \text{Proj}_g(\text{AvgPool}(\bF_8'))$.
$\bF_8'$ is upsampled and used as queries with $\bF_4^{\text{ctx}}$ as keys and values using $7{\times}7$ windowed attention for efficiency:
\begin{equation}
  \bF_4' = \text{WinCrossAttn}(Q = \text{Up}(\bF_8'),\; KV = \bF_4^{\text{ctx}}).
\end{equation}
Three refinement blocks (each: Conv $3{\times}3$ $\to$ GroupNorm $\to$ ReLU $\to$ Conv $3{\times}3$ $\to$ GroupNorm $\to$ ReLU $\to$ residual addition) progressively upsample from $1/4$ to full resolution.
Two $1{\times}1$ convolution heads produce saliency logits $\hat{\bM}_{\text{sal}}$ and boundary logits $\hat{\bM}_{\text{bnd}}$ at $384{\times}384$.

\subsection{Training Objective}
The objective is a weighted sum of the eight terms that are active in the reported configuration
(the Z-loss and the router-confidence term are disabled):
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
  \mathcal{L} =\;& \lambda_{\text{BCE}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}
  + \lambda_{\text{IoU}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{IoU}}
  + \lambda_{\text{SSIM}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{SSIM}}
  + \lambda_{\text{BND}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{BND}}\\
  &+ \lambda_{\text{LB}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{LB}}
  + \lambda_{\text{IMP}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{IMP}}
  + \lambda_{\text{auxBND}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{auxBND}}
  + \lambda_{\text{DS}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{DS}},
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
with $\lambda_{\text{BCE}}{=}\lambda_{\text{IoU}}{=}\lambda_{\text{SSIM}}{=}\lambda_{\text{BND}}{=}1.0$,
$\lambda_{\text{LB}}{=}\lambda_{\text{IMP}}{=}0.01$, $\lambda_{\text{auxBND}}{=}0.5$ and $\lambda_{\text{DS}}{=}0.4$ ($\lambda_{\text{LB}}$ is applied at each of the three scales).
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BCE}}$ is binary cross-entropy with logits between predicted saliency logits and ground-truth mask.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{IoU}}$ is the soft IoU loss:
$1 - \text{mean}_b\!\left[\frac{\sum_{hw}\sigma(\hat{M}_b) \cdot M_b + \epsilon}{\sum_{hw}\sigma(\hat{M}_b) + \sum_{hw} M_b - \sum_{hw}\sigma(\hat{M}_b) \cdot M_b + \epsilon}\right]$.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{SSIM}}$ is $1$ minus the structural-similarity index between the sigmoid prediction and the mask.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{BND}}$ is a smooth-$L_1$ term between the magnitude of the predicted spatial gradient and the ground-truth boundary map.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{LB}}$ is the load-balancing loss:
$\sum_{s} E \sum_j f_j^{(s)} P_j^{(s)}$, summed over the three scales, where $f_j$ is the fraction of all routing slots assigned to expert $j$, computed from hard assignments and detached, and $P_j$ is the differentiable mean gate mass.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{IMP}}$ is the importance loss:
$\frac{1}{|\mathcal{S}|}\sum_{s} (\text{std}(\bP^{(s)}) / \text{mean}(\bP^{(s)}))^2$, where $\bP^{(s)} \in \reals^E$ denotes the per-expert mean gate-mass vector at scale $s$.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{auxBND}}$ is binary cross-entropy between the boundary head's logits and the boundary map.
$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DS}}$ is the mean binary cross-entropy of the three per-scale auxiliary saliency heads; the auxiliary heads exist because deep supervision is enabled in the reported configuration.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Setup}
We evaluate on WXSOD~\cite{wxsod2025}, which provides 12,891 training images (scene-aware 80/20 split), 1,500 synthetic test images across 9 weather categories, and 554 real-world test images across 5 categories (fog, rain, snow, dark, low-light).
Images are aspect-preserving resized to $384{\times}384$; augmentations include horizontal flip ($p{=}0.5$), color jitter ($p{=}0.5$), and ImageNet normalization.
Training uses DDP on 2 GPUs, AMP (FP16), AdamW optimizer (lr $10^{-4}$ for both newly introduced modules and the backbone, weight decay $10^{-4}$), WarmupCosine schedule (1\% warmup, 50 epochs), gradient clipping at 1.0, effective batch size 32. The backbone is frozen during the first epoch and unfrozen from epoch 1 onward.
Metrics: MAE, $S_\phi$~\cite{struct-measure}, $E_\phi^{\text{adp}}$~\cite{enhanced-measure}, $F_\beta^{\max}$~\cite{f-measure}.
The model has 69.21M parameters and 277.9G MACs per $384{\times}384$ input image.

\noindent\textbf{What we find.} Four results, each backed by a table below.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep,topsep=2pt,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{The mixture-of-experts does not change the prediction.} Removing it entirely
        leaves real-test MAE at $0.0195$, identical to the full model, and routing at one
        pyramid scale instead of three gives the same $0.0195$ (Table~\ref{tab:ablate}).
  \item \textbf{Competitive without weather labels.} MAE $0.0192$ on the 1{,}500 synthesized
        test images leads every method in the published benchmark, including the
        weather-supervised WFANet ($0.0229$); $0.0168$ on the 554 real images is second of
        nineteen (Table~\ref{tab:compare}).
  \item \textbf{The router is not making a meaningful choice.} Routing entropy sits at
        $99.8$--$100\%$ of its $\log E$ ceiling in every arm, and expert weights are
        orthogonal while their activations correlate at $0.64$
        (Tables~\ref{tab:entropy} and~\ref{tab:diag_all}).
  \item \textbf{Training budget matters more than any design choice.} The same recipe at 14
        epochs instead of 8 improves real-test MAE from $0.0195$ to $0.0174$, against a seed
        spread of $\pm0.0004$ (Tables~\ref{tab:ablate} and~\ref{tab:seeds}).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Main Results}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports global performance on both test splits.
The model achieves MAE of $0.0192$ on synthetic data and $0.0168$ on real-world data.
Real-world MAE ($0.0168$) is slightly lower than synthetic MAE ($0.0192$). These numbers are
driven primarily by the backbone and the training schedule rather than by the routing
mechanism under study (Section~IV-E).
However, this comparison is confounded by test-set composition: the synthetic test set includes compound weather conditions (rain+fog, rain+snow, snow+fog) that are absent from the real-world test set, which may increase synthetic-test error independently of any domain-shift effect.

\subsection{Ablations}
Table~\ref{tab:ablate} reports every arm we trained.
The middle block shares one recipe --- renormalized gate, router noise enabled, effective
batch 32, 8 epochs --- and each row changes a single field, so those rows are directly
comparable to one another.

Three findings stand out.
First, \textbf{removing the mixture-of-experts entirely leaves performance unchanged}: the
no-MoE arm scores 0.0195 on real test against 0.0195 for the full model, with $S_\phi$
agreeing to 0.0010.
Second, \textbf{reducing routing from three pyramid scales to one changes nothing} (0.0195),
and replacing the routed experts with a single dense expert per scale changes nothing
(0.0191).
Third, the only architectural knob that moves the metric is top-$k$: routing to a single
expert costs 0.0017 MAE (0.0212 against 0.0195), roughly four times the seed spread of
$\pm0.0004$ measured across seeds 42--44.

(The A2, A4 and A5 deltas of $0.0002$, $0.0001$ and $0.0004$ sit inside that band and are not
meaningful; only the top-$k$ result at $0.0017$ and the epoch count at $0.0021$ exceed it.)

That floor is measured on the reference configuration, and the remaining arms are single-seed,
so their deltas are compared against a floor estimated elsewhere. A paired test over per-image
errors would settle this directly and is not reported here.

\textbf{Training budget is the only variable that matters more than any architectural choice}: the
same recipe and seed trained for 14 epochs instead of 8 improves real-test MAE from 0.0195
to 0.0174, a larger effect than every design-choice difference we measured combined.
We therefore report the architecture as characterisation, not as a demonstrated improvement.

\subsection{Why Routing Does Not Matter}
\textbf{Why does none of it matter?} Two measurements answer this, and together they are the
mechanistic contribution of the paper.

\emph{The router is indifferent.} Routing entropy sits at 99.8--100\% of its $\log E$
ceiling (Table~\ref{tab:entropy}), so the distribution over experts is essentially uniform:
the router almost never expresses a preference.

\emph{The experts are redundant.} Table~\ref{tab:sim} compares expert pairs at each scale.
Pairwise cosine similarity between expert \emph{weights} is essentially zero, so the experts
are not copies of one another. But cosine similarity between their \emph{activations} on real
data is 0.6424 at scale $1/4$, 0.2733 at $1/8$ and 0.8617 at $1/16$.
Despite different weights, the experts' outputs are substantially correlated at two of the
three scales, so for most tokens which expert they are routed to changes little.

Fig.~\ref{fig:qualitative} shows the same insensitivity qualitatively, and
Table~\ref{tab:diag_all} extends the similarity measurement to every arm.
This also explains why the one knob that matters is top-$k$: with $k{=}2$ the output averages
two correlated experts, suppressing their individual idiosyncrasies, whereas $k{=}1$ takes a
single expert's output and is measurably worse (0.0212 against 0.0195).

\subsection{Discussion}
The model demonstrates that spatial token-level routing is feasible for single-RGB SOD without weather-specific supervision.
However, several important limitations must be noted.
First and most centrally, the spatial MoE is shown across eleven trained arms and four
inference-time interventions to have no measurable effect on output quality; we treat that as
the paper's primary contribution rather than a caveat. Routing entropy also sits at
$99.8$--$100\%$ of its ceiling, so the decoder's entropy channel is nearly constant.
Second, the comparison against prior methods uses the benchmark's published numbers rather than our own re-implementations, so it is a comparison as reported; WFANet still attains the best real-split MAE, so we do not claim state-of-the-art performance.
Third, the reported model attains boundary F1 of only $0.3601$ on real test. That follows from
training with the boundary term inactive, and it is a limitation of the reported model rather
than of the ablation.
Fourth, the model was trained once without replicate runs, so variance estimates are unavailable.
Fifth, the forced-expert analysis suggests that expert specialization may be limited, and the benefit of multi-expert routing over a single larger expert remains unclear.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented and rigorously ablated SpatialMoE-SOD. The full model is competitive with
weather-supervised baselines on WXSOD -- MAE $0.0168$ on the 554 real test images and $0.0192$ on
the 1{,}500 synthetic ones -- but a complete ablation and inference-time intervention study shows
that the mixture-of-experts is not responsible for that performance: it can be removed, reduced to
one pyramid scale, or replaced with a single dense expert with no measurable effect. This
generalises to dense prediction a caution already familiar in the MoE literature, that sparse
routing does not guarantee learned specialisation.

The ablation matrix is otherwise complete: removing the mixture-of-experts, reducing routing to one
scale, replacing routed experts with a dense expert and removing the load-balancing loss all leave
real-test MAE within $0.0004$ of the reference, while routing to a single expert costs $0.0017$.
The mixture is therefore not earning its parameters on this task, and the useful direction is not
more routing capacity but the training budget.

Future work should determine whether a routing objective that is actually supervised -- rather than
one optimised only through the saliency loss -- can induce specialisation, and should re-run the
benchmark's baselines under a single unified protocol.
